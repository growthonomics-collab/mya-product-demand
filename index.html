Where every piece sits, BCG style

The classic growth-share matrix, filled with our own two numbers. Share = the Popularity Ratio in the product's main collection (1.00 = collection average). Growth = momentum, the 90-day ratio divided by the 12-month ratio (1.00 = holding its position). Newcomers with no 12-month history, which this season means the whole House of Peonies drop, are question marks by definition.

The formula
Demand Share % = 100 × [ 0.35 × Views ÷ ΣViews(collection) + 0.25 × Users ÷ ΣUsers(collection) + 0.15 × Engaged sessions ÷ ΣEngaged(collection) + 0.15 × Organic clicks ÷ ΣClicks(collection) + 0.10 × Add-to-cart ÷ ΣATC(collection) ]
Views and Users are recency-weighted: the last 30 days count double against days 31 to 90
Popularity Ratio = Demand Share % × Products in collection ÷ 100
Newcomers (under 90 days on site): Ratio = (real views × own ratio + 100 × 1.50) ÷ (real views + 100)
12-month check = same share on 12 months of GA4 (0.45 Views + 0.35 Users + 0.20 Engaged sessions); Trend = 90-day ratio ÷ 12-month ratio

Each product is scored against the other products actually listed in the same Shopify collection (membership read from the store's own collection listings, collections). Ratio 1.00 = an average product of that collection; 3.00 and above = Hero (featured), 1.50 to 2.99 = Strong (first rows), 0.50 to 1.49 = Average (default order), under 0.50 = Low (back of the grid). Engaged sessions carry the engagement rate into the score, so a page people bounce from earns less than one they stay on. The 12-month column shows whether the 90-day position is new or established: Rising = 90-day ratio at least 25% above the 12-month ratio, Fading = at least 20% below. Sources: GA4 Pages and screens (30 days, 90 days and 12 months), GA4 Ecommerce items, Search Console landing pages (through the GA4 link).

Where a product stands when it enters after the formula
  1. Day 1: ratio 1.50, label New. With no views the prior is all there is: first rows of its collection, no Hero slot, no risk of the back of the grid.
  2. Every day after: the prior fades as views arrive. Its signals are scaled to a full window and blended with the prior in proportion to its real views: at 100 views it is halfway to its own performance, at 1,000 views the prior is gone. A strong launch shows in days, a weak one drifts down gently.
  3. Day 90 onwards: the plain formula. Launch date comes from the Shopify publish date, checked against GA4 (a republished product with earlier history is treated as established).

The formula

Every product is scored inside its collection, never against the whole store, so a strong skirt is not buried under dresses. The score is a Demand Share: the percentage of the collection's total demand that one product captures. Shares inside a collection add up to 100.

Demand Share (product, collection) =
  0.35 × Views(product) / Σ Views(collection)
 + 0.25 × Users(product) / Σ Users(collection)
 + 0.15 × Engaged sessions(product) / Σ Engaged sessions(collection)
 + 0.15 × Organic clicks(product) / Σ Organic clicks(collection)
 + 0.10 × Add-to-cart(product) / Σ Add-to-cart(collection)
 × 100

Engaged sessions = GA4 sessions over 10 seconds, with a key event, or with 2+ page views
SignalSourceWeightWhy
Page viewsGA4 · Pages and screens35%Raw interest. Counts repeat looks, which is how people shop for occasion wear.
Active usersGA4 · Pages and screens25%Breadth of interest. Stops one visitor refreshing a page from inflating it.
Engaged sessionsGA4 · Pages and screens15%Quality of interest. A product people stay on, scroll and click earns more than one they bounce from.
Organic clicksSearch Console (GA4 link) · Landing pages15%Demand from Google, independent of where the store links internally. On this store most organic clicks land on collection pages, so the weight is redistributed in collections with no product-level clicks.
Add-to-cartGA4 · Ecommerce items10%Purchase intent. Low weight because sold-out products cannot be added to cart, which would otherwise punish the most wanted pieces.

If a collection has no data for a signal (for example no organic clicks yet), that weight is dropped and the others are rescaled, so the shares still add up to 100.

Reading the number

A Demand Share of 6.5 means that product captures 6.5% of everything the collection's shoppers do. Inside a collection of 53 dresses the average product sits at 1.9, so anything above 6 is a hero piece. Compare shares within a collection only; a 30% share in the 8-product pants range is not the same as 30% in Sirens dresses.

Purchases are shown but not scored: purchases across the period are enough for a tie-break and for checking that a popular product also sells, not for ranking a 459-product catalogue.

Engagement rate on its own

The tables also show each product's engagement rate. Site-wide, product pages run at about engaged sessions, against for the site as a whole. Read it as a quality check next to the ratio: a Hero product with an engagement rate well under the collection average is being clicked on but not liked (price, imagery or a mismatch with the listing it came from), and a Low product with a very high engagement rate is a candidate for better placement.

Stock, and why it changes the reading

Collections, not URL words

Shopify already knows which products belong to which collection. The collections on the store were read from the store's own collection listings, so a product is ranked inside the collections that actually list it, in the same order the customer sees. Each product has a main collection for its headline ratio: the season sub-collection in the menu (for example Sirens · Dresses) when it has one, otherwise the season root, otherwise the largest collection it belongs to.

Three kinds of collection appear in the Rankings selector: the menu collections (starred), the wedding and christening landing pages that exist for Google rather than the menu, and tag, drop and older collections that still carry traffic. Collection pages that have views but no live products (last season's drops, swimwear) are listed under Collections with their traffic only.

Recency and the 12-month check

Views and users are recency-weighted with a two-step rule: the last 30 days count double against days 31 to 90, normalised back to the 90-day scale. A product rising this month therefore shows now rather than at the next quarterly refresh. The 12-month column is the same share formula over 12 months of GA4 (23 September 2025 to 22 September 2026) using views, users and engaged sessions; Search Console and add-to-cart are not part of it because the organic export covers the current period only.

The Trend flag compares the two: Rising means the 90-day ratio is at least 25% above the 12-month ratio, Fading means at least 20% below, Steady is anything between, New means it has 90-day data but no earlier history. Remember the calendar when reading Fading: the 12 months include the wedding and christening season peak (spring and early summer), the 90 days run from late June to late September, so a summer occasion dress can look like it is fading in September when it is simply between seasons.

    Refreshing the data

    1. GA4 → Reports → Engagement → Pages and screens, dimension Page path and screen class. Customise the report (pencil) and add Sessions, Engaged sessions, Engagement rate, Add to carts and Ecommerce purchases, Apply. Export three date ranges: last 30 days, last 90 days, last 12 months (Share → Download CSV).
    2. GA4 → Reports → Monetization → Ecommerce purchases (Item name), last 90 days, Download CSV.
    3. GA4 → Reports → Search Console → Google organic search traffic (Landing page + query string), last 90 days, Download CSV.
    4. Run the build script. It re-crawls the store's collections and products (Shopify's public collections.json and products.json), recomputes Demand Share and regenerates this page with the current stock status.

    When a new product enters the store

    A product that goes live after the formula has run has no history, so a plain share-of-collection score would give it close to 0 and push it to the back of its collection. That is the wrong signal: no data means unknown, not unpopular. The scoring handles this itself. It reads each product's launch date from Shopify (published_at), checks it against GA4 (a republished product with earlier views is treated as established), and applies the three-stage rule below before ranking.

    Launch checklist

    1. Assign it to its season root and its type sub-collection on day one (for example House of Peonies FW27 and Peonies · Dresses). The scoring ranks it only inside the collections that list it.
    2. Set the provisional ratio of 1.50 and the "New" flag in the product metafields so the collection sort picks it up immediately.
    3. Give it two internal links from the collection it belongs to (collection description or a "New in" block) so it collects organic clicks from the start.
    4. At day 30 run the score. Hero or Strong: move it into the earned slot and drop the "New" label. Average or Low: it keeps the default position, and the merchandising question becomes price, imagery or duplication with an existing best seller.

    Using it on the store

    1. Collection ordering. Put the top five Demand Share products first on each collection page (Shopify manual sort, or a metafield sort through an app). Revisit monthly during a launch, quarterly otherwise.
    2. Internal linking. From each collection page, link to its top three products in the collection description, and from those products back to the collection.
    3. Stock first. Filter Products by Stock = Sold out and Tier = Hero or Strong: these are the pieces to restock, re-cut or replace with the closest in-stock alternative, and the pages to add a back-in-stock signup to.
    4. Merchandising gaps. Where the Collections tab shows a collection page with strong views but its products carry low Demand Share, the page is sending traffic to pieces people do not want to look at. Refresh the selection there first.
